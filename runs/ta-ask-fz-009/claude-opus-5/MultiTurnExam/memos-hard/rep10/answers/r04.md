### The actual mechanism: an in-process Go channel

Webhook delivery uses a **bounded buffered channel with a fixed goroutine pool**, entirely inside the server process (`internal/webhook/webhook.go`):

- **The "queue"** is `asyncPostQueue = make(chan *WebhookRequestPayload, 128)` — line 39. That's the declaration site, and its capacity of **128** is the entire buffer.
- **The "workers"** are started in `init()` (lines 42–55): `for range 4` spawns **4 goroutines**, each ranging over the channel and calling `Post(payload)` (line 46), logging failures with `slog.Warn` (lines 47–50).
- **The producer** is `PostAsync` (line 184), which does a non-blocking send via `select` with a `default` branch (lines 189–195).
- **Delivery** executes at `safeClient.Do(req)` in `Post` (line 152).

### Why the distinction matters operationally

An in-process channel is not a broker, and the differences are load-bearing:

1. **No durability.** The queue lives in RAM. A restart or crash loses every pending webhook — there's no persisted backlog to resume.
2. **No retries.** `Post` returns an error which the worker only logs (lines 47–50). A 500 from the receiver means the event is gone. A broker's redelivery semantics simply don't exist here.
3. **Silent drops under load.** The `default` case (lines 191–195) discards the payload with a warning when all 128 slots are full — backpressure is shed rather than propagated, so the RPC caller never learns.
4. **No cross-instance coordination.** Each server process has its own channel and its own 4 workers, so nothing is shared or balanced across replicas.

The receiver-side design does follow broker-adjacent conventions — Standard Webhooks HMAC signing with `webhook-id`, `webhook-timestamp`, `webhook-signature` headers (lines 147–149), where `webhook-id` gives receivers an idempotency key. But that's a wire-format convention, not evidence of a broker behind it.
